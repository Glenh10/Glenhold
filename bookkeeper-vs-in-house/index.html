Outsourced Bookkeeper vs. Hiring an In-House Bookkeeper

An employee and an outsourced bookkeeper solve different problems. The right one depends on how much bookkeeping you actually have.

Book a Free Call

Start with the job, not the title

Owners ask me whether they should hire someone or send the books out. The useful question underneath is how many days of bookkeeping the business really has. A monthly close is one kind of job. A person who should be in the office all week, paying bills and answering customers, is another.

I do the first kind. You work directly with me. If you need the second kind, I'll tell you that an employee is the better fit.

What outsourced bookkeeping is good at

For a lot of contractors, home-service companies, and professional services firms, the close happens in a couple of sittings. The rest of the month you're on the job. A salary still runs on the days nobody is in the books.

The pattern I see next is the bookkeeping seat collecting the phones, the scheduling, and the supply orders, because those are visible. The reconciliation is what slips. Monthly bookkeeping with me has one job, and the questions come to me.

When an in-house bookkeeper is the better hire

Hire in-house if someone needs to be physically present. Typical signs:

  • Bills should be entered and paid throughout the week, not in a monthly batch.
  • Customers walk in or call with payment questions that should be answered on the spot.
  • You want a person at a desk your crew already knows.
  • The bookkeeping, billing, and office work together really do fill the week.

That's a real job. If those days are real, hire for the office. I can catch the books up first so a new hire isn't spending their first quarter digging through old months.

The costs people forget

The number that rarely shows up in a job posting is your time. You write the role, interview, train someone on your file, and then review their work. While that seat is empty, or while they're out, the month still has to close.

An outsourced plan has limits of its own. I'm not in your office, and I won't run the front desk. I need statements and answers from you, and the close waits on those. If it isn't working, you can stop without unwinding a hire. We agree on the scope before I start, and if monthly work ends, you keep the QuickBooks file and the reports.

Tax return preparation and filing sit outside both options unless the person you hire also does that work. I don't file returns. I keep books your tax preparer can rely on.

How I would choose

If the books need a few focused days a month, start with a monthly plan and skip the job posting. If you're already turning work away because nobody is in the office to handle money questions, hire. If you're unsure, tell me what a normal week looks like and I'll give you an honest read.

Common questions

Can the monthly scope change without hiring or letting someone go?

Yes. If you add an account, turn on payroll, or the transaction count jumps, we change the monthly scope. You aren't posting a job, or cutting one, to make that adjustment.

Do you pay bills or take customer payment calls?

No. I close the books. Paying bills through the week, collecting from customers, and answering people at the counter are office work. If that's the job you need filled, you want someone on site.

What would a later hire inherit?

Your QuickBooks file, the last reconciled month, and a list of anything still open. They shouldn't have to guess which balances were already tied out.
